Most clients come to us knowing they need "good renders". Fewer know exactly what they are buying, or what distinguishes a useful visualization from an expensive one. This guide covers the practical side — not technical detail, but the decisions that affect budget, timeline and outcome. It was rewritten this year because the economics changed: the polishing stretch that used to dominate the schedule is now largely an AI pass, and that moves both the price and what you should ask for.

## What you are actually buying

Good visualization is a decision-support tool. The obvious output is images. The actual value is faster stakeholder alignment, early problem detection, and marketing material that does its job.

A render that exposes a design issue before construction begins has already paid for itself. A visualization that helps a client understand a space — and commit to it — compresses the decision cycle. That is what we are building; the pictures are how it ships.

## The main types and when to use them

**Still images** are the foundation. Photorealistic views of your project — exterior, interior, aerial. They work for planning submissions, marketing collateral, presentations. Priced per view on our [price list](/pricing/), with the 3D model as a separate one-off line. Timeline: 2–5 days per image set once the model exists.

**Variants of a view** are the new cheap thing. A night or dusk version, a different season, a material option, a different furnishing style — generated from the approved view by our AI pipeline with geometry and camera locked, at a fraction of a new view. Ask for them; they used to cost almost as much as the original.

**AI images** are a category of their own now: a photorealistic image from any 2D input — a sketch, a flat software export, an old render — and AI-furnished interiors from an empty 3D model. Lower cost per image than a modelled-and-rendered view, and no viewpoint you cannot see in the input. Right for concept stages and for filling a sales sheet with a finished picture of every flat type.

**360° panoramic tours** let viewers look around a rendered space interactively — more engaging than stills for space-planning reviews and sales presentations, and they work on the web and in headsets. Priced per viewpoint, generated from the same 3D scene as the stills. Timeline: 3–7 days on top of the model.

**Animation** shows movement through a space — flythroughs, walkthroughs, aerial sequences. Essential when sequence and flow matter more than a frozen moment. Two grades: a full 3D animation, priced per second and rendered frame by frame from the model; and AI motion from stills, a fraction of that price, right for social loops and sales-office screens. Timeline: 1–3 weeks for a standard 3D piece; days for AI motion.

**Interactive, web and VR** run in real time — a web 3D viewer with variant sets, a configurator, a guided-tour presenter mode for the sales office, or a full Unreal Engine VR application. Best for high-value sales scenarios and detailed design review. Timeline: 2–6 weeks; VR applications are quoted per project.

## Quality levels

Not every project needs the same quality. Matching the output to the purpose keeps costs appropriate.

**Basic** makes sense for early design phases, permitting and competitions — a correct, clean render without the finishing pass. Quick, lower cost, appropriate for decisions that will change anyway.

**Standard** covers most business needs — planning submissions, developer marketing, investor presentations. The finishing pass is included: this is where the cost/quality ratio is best, and where AI made the biggest difference.

**Premium** is for high-profile marketing, signature projects, or material that has to hold up over a long campaign — hand-dressed interiors, specified materials, more attention to every surface, and the AI pass used for candidates rather than for the final polish.

## What drives the cost

**The model.** The single biggest line, and the one AI does not touch. It scales with the building — a house, a block, a masterplan — and with your input: a supplied 3D model halves it, a PDF or a sketch doubles it.

**Number of views.** More camera angles mean more setup, rendering and review. Variants of an existing view are cheap; new views are not.

**Interiors.** Priced per square metre of model, with a furnished flat costing roughly twice an empty one — which is why AI interiors from the empty model exist as a middle option.

**Timeline.** Standard timelines allow efficient scheduling; rush work carries a fixed multiplier and compresses the iteration cycle.

**Animation vs. stills.** Frame-by-frame 3D animation is priced per second and needs real render time. AI motion is priced per second too, at a fraction of the complex-animation rate.

**How complete your input is.** Clean CAD files, clear material specifications and good reference images mean less ambiguity and fewer corrections. Rough sketches and verbal descriptions work, but add iteration.

## What AI changed, and what it did not

It changed the last stretch. Finishing, variants, interiors for sales sheets, upscaling to print, motion for social — all faster and cheaper than in 2024, and available as separate line items. It did not change the model, the camera decisions, or the judgement about what an image needs to say. If a supplier tells you the whole thing is now "AI-generated" and priced accordingly, ask them where the geometry comes from. Our own answer, in detail: [AI in Architectural Visualization: What's Actually Useful](/articles/ai-in-architectural-visualization/).

One more thing to ask any supplier: whether the AI tools they use are licensed for commercial delivery, and whether your unbuilt project is being uploaded to a third-party service. Ours run on our own hardware, and each delivered file records the tools that touched it.

## What keeps costs down

A clear brief upfront. Materials decided before modelling starts. A flexible timeline. Reusing the same 3D model across output types — the model built for stills becomes the basis for the 360° tour, the animation and the web viewer. Batching views from the same scene. Asking for variants where a variant will do.
