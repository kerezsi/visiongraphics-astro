This one is a side project, and we are labelling it as such before the first paragraph ends. It is not a service, it is not on the price list, and we are not offering to score anyone's film. It is a prototype on one machine in the studio, and it is worth writing up only because building it taught us things about local models that transfer to the work we are paid for.

The itch is real enough. An architectural film gets cut, graded and approved, and then it needs thirty to ninety seconds of music. The options are a stock library — pay per project, and spend an afternoon rejecting the same six corporate-uplifting tracks everyone else is using — or a composer, which is right for a big film and overkill for a lobby loop. Which licence covers which use is a recurring small annoyance rather than a crisis: exactly the shape of problem a studio starts poking at on a Sunday.

**The transferable part is the discipline, not the music.** We already run image models entirely on our own hardware, as much for client confidentiality as for cost. Audio turns out to be the same engineering problem in a different hat: a handful of open models, each with its own Python environment and its own idea of what a file is, and a great deal of unglamorous plumbing between them. Getting the plumbing right is the whole job — it was on the image side too.

## The stack

The tool is a single Flask server on one port, serving nine static pages and wrapping every model behind a small HTTP API. No build step, no npm, no database. Generated files land in an `output/` folder, one directory per job.

The generation side pulls in what is currently available and open:

- **Symbolic orchestration.** SymphonyGen — a 3D hierarchical model conditioned on a beat-quantised harmony skeleton, accepted at ISMIR 2026 — writes full orchestral MIDI either from scratch or from a chord progression you hand it. The server exposes three of its released checkpoints and up to four variations per run.
- **Text to MIDI.** text2midi from the AMAAI Lab turns a written description ("epic orchestral piece in D minor with french horns, timpani and full strings") into a MIDI sketch.
- **Continuation and accompaniment.** Stanford's Anticipatory Music Transformer either keeps composing where a MIDI file stops, or treats it as a melody and writes parts around it.
- **Audio generation.** ACE-Step 1.5 runs as a local subprocess and produces actual audio from a prompt, with declared BPM and key.
- **Analysis, going the other way.** Basic Pitch transcribes audio back to MIDI, Demucs splits a track into four stems, Matchering masters a mix against a reference.
- **Sound.** One soft synth for the whole app: js-synthesizer 1.13.0 with libfluidsynth 2.4.6 compiled to WebAssembly, loading the same GeneralUser GS soundfont that the server-side FluidSynth renders with. Sixteen MIDI channels, one stereo output — so per-track level and pan are CC7 and CC10 rather than a Web Audio gain node that cannot exist.

The models above all run on the machine, with two exceptions: Magenta's GrooVAE fetches its drum checkpoints from Google's CDN on first use, and the tool pages load their MIDI-player library from a CDN rather than vendoring it.

## The pages on top

The front page is a music sketchpad — chord progression editor, melody lane, drum grid, key and scale selection across sixty-two scales, cadence labelling, reharmonisation suggestions, negative-harmony mirroring, a lyrics lane, and Web MIDI in and out so a keyboard or a DAW can join in.

![The sketchpad: chord progression blocks along the bottom, melody lane above, scale keyboard at the side](/_img/articles/music-workbench/sketchpad-progression.jpg)

Attached to it is the piece nobody needed: a world map. Scales, drum patterns and time signatures are pinned to where they come from, and a Songs tab carries fifty-five traditional songs from forty-six countries and regions, each loadable into the sketchpad as a working progression with its melody. It began as a way to make an unfamiliar scale mean something.

![The world atlas — scales, beats, time signatures and traditional songs pinned to a map](/_img/articles/music-workbench/atlas-map.jpg)

From there the material moves to a song editor: a canvas timeline with MIDI and audio tracks, clips you can move, trim, split and loop, waveform peaks drawn per clip, a metronome and count-in, project save and undo. The transport runs on integer ticks at 480 per quarter note, with a 25 ms scheduler filling a 150 ms lookahead window — the standard two-clocks arrangement, which is what makes editing during playback free.

![The song editor arrangement view — MIDI and audio clips on a canvas timeline with waveform peaks](/_img/articles/music-workbench/song-editor-arrangement.jpg)

The same page carries a second view over the same model: a session grid, borrowed wholesale from Ableton's behaviour rather than reinvented. One playing clip per track, launches quantised to a bar or a beat, scenes as rows, a QWERTY grid across four scenes and eight tracks. Bouncing renders the MIDI tracks server-side with FluidSynth, sums them against the audio clips in an `OfflineAudioContext`, and drops the mix back into `output/`.

![The session view — clip slots in a grid, scenes as rows, quantised launch](/_img/articles/music-workbench/session-grid.jpg)

## The output folder is the message bus

The one design decision we would defend is the least clever one. Every producer writes a job directory with a small `manifest.json` — what made it, when, from which parent job, and whatever metadata it knows, like BPM and key. Every consumer accepts a path relative to `output/` instead of demanding an upload. That is the entire interoperability layer: files as IPC, no database, no schema registry.

What it buys is disproportionate. A picker lists recent jobs with their provenance chain, so "stems ← song ← sketch" reads at a glance. Every result grows a "Send to" menu whose targets are one line each in a routing table. A `BroadcastChannel` lets a result finished in one tab announce itself to the others, so the song editor can offer to import it instead of losing it.

![The file picker — recent jobs with provenance chains, filtered to MIDI or audio](/_img/articles/music-workbench/output-picker.jpg)

## The honest position

This is a prototype, and the hand-offs are still its weak point.

The design note that drove the current round opened by listing the damage: five mutually incompatible note formats in circulation, every hand-off one-way out of the sketchpad, and three tools that were outright dead ends — the drum page never touched the server, generated audio never landed in the shared folder, and the orchestrator's variations fed nothing. Those three are closed. What replaced them is not a finished system, it is a convention that everyone currently obeys.

The unfinished parts are specific. The sketchpad was deliberately not migrated onto the shared playback modules, so the app carries two copies of the timing code. The song editor edits clips, not notes — there is no piano roll in it, and anything below clip level goes back to the sketchpad to be edited. Audio follows tempo by changing playback rate, which also changes pitch; proper time-stretching is a planned dependency nobody has needed enough to add. Above fifteen melodic tracks, they start sharing a MIDI channel, because one synth instance has sixteen. A harmony skeleton is silently looped or truncated to exactly thirty-two bars. Only one heavy job runs at a time, because there is one GPU and a single lock guarding it. Leave the tab in the background and the browser throttles the scheduler until playback starves.

None of that is a bug list waiting to be cleared. They are ceilings chosen on purpose to get something working, each written down next to the code that imposes it. The thing plays, it generates, it round-trips, and it is nowhere near being a product.

Nothing from it has gone into a delivered film. It may never. What it has produced is a much better feel for how far local audio models have come, where they still fall over, and how much of "AI tooling" is actually file management — roughly the answer the image side gave too.
